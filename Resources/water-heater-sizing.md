# What Size Water Heater Do I Need?

The wrong water heater can be too small while carrying the “right” gallon number.

Size depends on when your household uses hot water, not only how many people live in the home. Storage heaters combine tank volume, first-hour rating, and recovery. Tankless heaters depend on simultaneous flow and the temperature rise from incoming water to the hot-water setting. Space, fuel, electrical capacity, venting, airflow, condensate, and manufacturer limits can rule out equipment that looks large enough on paper.

Household count gives you a place to begin. Peak demand decides what the heater has to do.

## Start with the busiest hour

Think about the heaviest realistic period of hot-water use, often a morning or evening window.

Which fixtures and appliances run? Which ones overlap? How long do the showers last? Does the household regularly start laundry or a dishwasher while people are bathing? Did a larger tub, body-spray shower, accessory dwelling unit, or new occupant change the pattern?

Write down the actual sequence instead of choosing a tank from a “family of four” chart. Four people who shower at different times create a different load from four people getting ready in the same hour.

The U.S. Department of Energy’s purchasing guidance treats anticipated household hot-water use as a selection input. That principle matters more than the shortcut.

## Storage sizing uses more than tank gallons

A storage heater begins with a reserve of hot water. While you use that reserve, the heater begins recovering.

Three measurements work together:

### Tank volume

Tank volume tells you how much water the vessel holds. It does not tell you how much usable hot water the system can deliver during the busiest hour.

Stored water mixes with incoming cold water as hot water leaves. The thermostat setting, inlet temperature, burner or element input, tank design, and use pattern change what happens next.

### First-hour rating

First-hour rating estimates how much hot water a fully heated storage water heater can deliver in a busy first hour. It includes the stored water and the hot water produced while the tank recovers.

That is why two units with the same nominal tank size can perform differently.

Read [what first-hour rating means](/resources/first-hour-rating) before comparing storage models.

### Recovery

Recovery describes how quickly the heater can replace heat after hot water is drawn. Burner or element input, efficiency, tank size, inlet temperature, and thermostat setting all affect it.

A larger tank with slow recovery can behave differently from a smaller tank with fast recovery. The [recovery-time guide](/resources/water-heater-recovery-time) explains how to read that difference without relying on a generic wait-time chart.

## Heat-pump water heaters add operating mode and site conditions

An integrated heat-pump water heater is still a storage system, so tank volume, first-hour delivery, and peak demand matter. Its operating modes matter too.

DOE guidance explains that heat-pump modes can trade efficiency against recovery and resistance-element use. Mode names and behavior vary by model. A larger tank can sometimes help a household store more water and rely less on backup resistance heat, but that decision must come from the actual performance data and use pattern.

The location can also rule out the product. ENERGY STAR gives general guidance for surrounding air volume, ambient temperature, noise, and placement. The exact installation manual controls clearances, ducting, airflow, condensate, electrical, and siting requirements.

Do not size the tank before checking whether the heater can work in the room.

Read the existing [heat-pump water-heater guide](/water-heaters/heat-pump) for the full fit decision.

## Tankless sizing uses flow and temperature rise

Tankless systems do not run out of stored gallons. They reach a maximum rate at which they can heat moving water under a specific temperature rise.

Two facts determine the load:

### Simultaneous hot-water flow

Add the hot-water flow of the fixtures expected to run together. Do not add every fixture in the house unless that is a real use case.

Fixture labels and measurements can help, but mixed-water flow at a shower is not always the same as hot-water flow through the heater. A qualified designer or installer should confirm the demand used for final equipment selection.

### Design temperature rise

Temperature rise is the difference between incoming cold-water temperature and the desired hot-water output.

If incoming water is colder, the same tankless heater delivers less flow at the target temperature. A headline maximum gallons-per-minute figure often reflects a smaller temperature rise than the home’s hardest condition.

Ask for the manufacturer’s flow at the cold-end design rise used for your home. Get the value and assumption in writing.

The existing [gas tankless guide](/water-heaters/tankless) explains this problem in more detail, and [when tankless is not your best choice](/water-heaters/tankless/not-right-for-you) covers the installation and maintenance conditions that can rule it out.

## Capacity is only one filter

A heater can meet the hot-water calculation and still be wrong for the house.

Check these constraints before choosing a model:

- Fuel type and available gas capacity.
- Electrical voltage, circuit, service, and panel capacity.
- Venting route and combustion-air requirements.
- Room volume, airflow, ducting, temperature, and noise.
- Drain pan, drain route, condensate, and leak exposure.
- Equipment dimensions, clearances, access, and structural support.
- Water conditions and required maintenance.
- Qualified local service, parts, and warranty support.
- Permit, inspection, and local authority requirements.

These are elimination criteria. Preferences come later.

## Can a water heater be too large?

Oversizing can increase purchase cost, consume space, complicate installation, or move a product away from the operating pattern you intended. The consequences differ by technology and model.

Undersizing is easier to feel because the household runs out of capacity or exceeds the available flow. Oversizing can hide behind “more is safer” even when the home paid for capacity it cannot use.

The goal is adequate performance at the real peak, with a reasonable margin supported by the manufacturer’s data and the installation.

## Information to bring into the sizing conversation

Collect the facts a household chart cannot know:

- Current heater type, model, tank size, and performance rating.
- What runs during the busiest hot-water period.
- Whether the current system runs out, recovers slowly, or has always met demand.
- Shower, tub, appliance, recirculation, and special-use details.
- Current fuel, electrical service, panel location, gas line, venting, and installation space.
- Winter inlet-water assumption used for tankless design.
- Planned changes in occupants, fixtures, or use.
- Local utility territory and known rates or verified programs.

Ask the installer to show how the recommended model meets the demand. “We always install this size for a family of four” is not the calculation.
