# What Temperature Should My Water Heater Be Set To?

A single thermostat number cannot protect every person from every water risk.

A common household setting is 120°F, and federal consumer guidance has long used that number to reduce scald risk and energy use. Higher stored-water temperatures can increase scald danger. Public-health guidance also explains that lower temperatures can create different concerns for Legionella growth, especially when vulnerable occupants, system design, stagnation, or local requirements change the risk.

Use 120°F as a conversation starting point. Confirm the exact system, measured tap temperature, occupants, manufacturer instructions, and local requirements before changing anything.

## Why the advice appears to conflict

Scald prevention and microbial control pull in different directions.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission publishes guidance on tap-water scalds. EPA guidance on Legionella says temperature recommendations must also consider people at increased risk, state and local codes, and controls such as thermostatic mixing valves.

Those sources are answering related safety questions from different sides. Flattening them into one universal number hides the condition that matters most in a particular home.

This page does not choose a medical or code answer for you. It shows what must be checked before the decision is safe.

## Start with the people in the home

Scald risk is especially serious for young children, older adults, and people with reduced mobility, sensation, or ability to respond quickly. Health conditions or immune vulnerability can also change the conversation about water-system management.

If anyone in the household is medically vulnerable, use guidance from the person’s qualified healthcare professional together with a licensed plumbing professional who understands the home’s hot-water system. Do not treat a general web article as individualized health advice.

## Stored temperature and tap temperature are not always the same

The thermostat setting, temperature inside the tank, and temperature delivered at a fixture can differ.

Heat loss through piping, distance, recirculation, fixture controls, mixing devices, equipment accuracy, and operating conditions can change what arrives at the tap. A number printed on a dial may also be approximate rather than a calibrated measurement.

This distinction matters because some systems manage stored-water temperature and delivered-water temperature separately. The design and installation of those controls belongs with a qualified professional and the local requirements.

Do not install, adjust, or bypass a mixing valve or other safety control from a generic article.

## Measure the experience without opening the heater

If the system is operating normally and no urgent hazard exists, a homeowner can record the temperature delivered at a fixture using a suitable thermometer and a method approved by the relevant product or safety guidance.

Use caution around hot water. Keep children and vulnerable occupants away during measurement. Do not remove access panels, work on live electrical equipment, adjust gas controls, or operate pressure-relief equipment.

Record where and when the measurement was taken, whether recirculation was active, and whether the temperature changes across fixtures or over time. A qualified professional can use that information to compare the delivered result with the heater setting and system design.

## Check the manufacturer’s instructions

Water heaters do not all use the same controls.

Some have a marked digital setting. Others use approximate labels or concealed controls. Heat-pump water heaters can combine temperature with operating modes. Tankless systems may interact with recirculation and fixtures differently from storage systems.

Use the current manual for the exact model. If the instructions require opening a panel, working near gas or electricity, or altering a safety device, stop and use qualified service.

## Check local requirements and system conditions

Temperature decisions can be affected by:

- State or local code requirements.
- Healthcare or facility requirements.
- Recirculation and distribution design.
- Thermostatic mixing controls.
- Appliances that need a particular inlet temperature.
- Stagnation or low-use branches.
- The heater type and manufacturer instructions.
- Vulnerable occupants.

A homeowner in a simple, regularly used single-family system may face a different decision from a home with long distribution runs, recirculation, an accessory unit, intermittent occupancy, or a medically vulnerable resident.

## Do not use temperature to hide another problem

Raising the setting can make a small tank appear to deliver more mixed water at the fixtures. It also changes scald risk, energy use, and system conditions.

If the household is running out of hot water, diagnose demand, recovery, operating mode, and equipment performance before turning temperature into a capacity workaround. Read [why you are running out of hot water](/resources/running-out-of-hot-water) and the [water-heater sizing guide](/resources/water-heater-sizing).

If the temperature changed without anyone adjusting the unit, arrange model-specific inspection rather than assuming the dial drifted.

## The safe next step

Use the current product manual and measure what the household actually receives. Then bring these facts to a qualified professional when health risk, vulnerable occupants, local code, mixing controls, recirculation, or a changed temperature is involved.

This page intentionally does not use the system quiz as its primary call to action. A recommendation tool cannot settle a scald-versus-health risk for a particular household.

For routine ownership planning after the temperature decision is settled, use the [water-heater maintenance schedule](/resources/water-heater-maintenance).
