# Should I Repair or Replace My Water Heater?

A cheap repair can be expensive when it buys a few months before the tank leaks. A full replacement can be wasteful when one serviceable component failed on an otherwise sound unit.

Repair usually makes sense when the problem is isolated, the tank itself is sound, parts and qualified service are available, and the current water heater still fits the home. Replacement becomes more sensible when the tank is leaking, failures keep returning, coverage is gone, damage exposure is high, or the existing system no longer meets the household’s needs.

Age changes the odds. It does not settle the decision.

## Handle an active leak or safety concern first

If water is actively leaking, you smell gas, a carbon-monoxide alarm sounds, or the unit shows signs of overheating or electrical danger, stop comparing repair prices.

Go to the [water-heater emergency guide](/emergency). Follow the safe immediate actions that fit the situation and contact emergency services, the utility, or a licensed professional as directed.

This article begins after the immediate risk is controlled.

## The five facts that decide the answer

Most repair-or-replace advice starts with a birthday. “If the heater is more than X years old, replace it.” That is easy to remember. It is also too crude to spend thousands of dollars on.

Use five facts instead.

### 1. Where did the failure occur?

A serviceable control, heating element, igniter, sensor, or external connection creates a different decision than a leaking steel tank or damaged heat exchanger.

You still need an exact diagnosis. Water near the heater can come from the tank, a fitting, another appliance, a drain, or condensation. A symptom points to the next inspection. It does not prove which part failed.

Ask the person diagnosing the problem to name the failed component in writing and explain whether the failure affected the pressure vessel, combustion system, electrical system, or another replaceable part.

### 2. What condition is the rest of the system in?

One repaired part does not renew the rest of the heater.

Look at the repair history, visible corrosion, changed performance, repeated shutdowns, sediment or scale history, venting condition, drain protection, and the installation around the unit. The goal is not to turn a homeowner into a technician. The goal is to learn whether the proposed repair addresses one contained problem or sits inside a larger pattern.

### 3. What does the warranty actually cover?

The warranty headline rarely tells the whole story.

Coverage can differ for the tank, heat exchanger, parts, and labor. It can also depend on registration, installation, maintenance, water conditions, approved use, and who performs the work. Read the warranty for the exact model and serial number. Then confirm who pays for diagnosis, shipping, labor, removal, and reinstall work.

A “covered part” can still leave a meaningful labor bill.

### 4. What does the repair buy you?

Do not compare a repair invoice with the equipment price shown online. Compare the repair with the complete installed replacement that fits the same home.

The written repair option should include:

- The diagnosed failure and work being performed.
- Parts, labor, permit or inspection requirements, and warranty on the repair.
- Known work that remains outside the repair.
- Expected downtime and whether another failure would repeat the same labor.
- Any reason the technician believes replacement is already near.

There is no honest universal percentage where repair suddenly becomes foolish. A $900 repair on a young covered unit can make sense. A smaller repair on a tank above finished living space can carry more risk than the invoice suggests.

### 5. Does the current system still fit the home?

A failure creates a decision window. Sometimes the right move is the fastest like-for-like replacement. Sometimes it is the first practical chance to fix a system that has always run out of hot water, occupied valuable space, cost too much to operate, or depended on equipment the home cannot support well.

Check the current fit before approving either path:

- Does the household regularly run out of hot water?
- Has the number of occupants or peak-use pattern changed?
- Would a new technology require gas, venting, electrical, airflow, drain, or condensate work?
- Is the heater in a location where a tank leak could cause major damage?
- Are qualified service and parts available for the proposed equipment?
- Do current local rates or verified incentives change the ownership cost?

The [water-heater technology comparison](/water-heaters) explains the four main system choices. The [sizing guide](/resources/water-heater-sizing) shows why tank gallons alone do not answer the capacity question.

## A practical repair-or-replace rule

Use this as a decision frame, then confirm the diagnosis and scope with a qualified professional.

| Situation | Direction | What still needs verification |
|---|---|---|
| A contained, replaceable part failed; tank or heat exchanger is sound; current system fits | Repair remains a serious option | Exact diagnosis, part/labor coverage, remaining condition |
| Failure is uncertain or water source is unclear | Inspect before deciding | Leak source, model, age, warranty, safety condition |
| Tank or pressure vessel is leaking | Replacement is generally the planning path | Safe shutdown, complete replacement scope, damage control |
| Repairs repeat or several systems are deteriorating | Price replacement alongside repair | Root cause, code corrections, technology fit |
| Current unit cannot meet household demand | Repair may restore operation without solving the real problem | Proper size, recovery, peak demand, site constraints |
| Conversion work is extensive and hot water is needed immediately | A like-for-like replacement may be the practical choice | Downtime, temporary options, full conversion cost |

## What to collect before you approve the job

Take exterior photos without removing covers. Record the model and serial number, approximate installation date, warranty documents, service history, where water or corrosion appeared, when performance changed, and every line in the written diagnosis.

Then request two separate scopes when both are plausible:

1. Repair the diagnosed problem and state what is excluded.
2. Replace the system with a suitable option and itemize the work.
