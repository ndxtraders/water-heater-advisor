# How Do I Know My Water Heater Is Going Bad?

A water heater rarely sends one clean message that says, “Replace me.” It sends changes.

An active tank leak, a carbon-monoxide alarm, a gas odor, signs of overheating, or electrical danger needs immediate action. Use the [emergency guide](/emergency) and contact the appropriate emergency service, utility, or licensed professional. Other changes, including less hot water, repeated shutdowns, corrosion, unusual water, or new noise, justify inspection. None of them proves the whole heater has failed on its own.

Your first job is to notice the change without turning observation into hazardous repair work.

## Warning signs that need immediate action

### A carbon-monoxide alarm or combustion concern

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission describes carbon monoxide as a deadly, colorless, odorless gas and lists malfunctioning fuel-burning appliances, including water heaters, as possible sources.

Do not search for the source while remaining in a dangerous space. Follow the alarm instructions, get to fresh air, contact emergency services or the fuel utility as appropriate, and have the equipment evaluated by a qualified professional.

### An active leak or water spreading from the heater area

Water near a heater can come from the tank, a connection, a drain, another appliance, or condensation. The source changes the repair decision. The immediate concern is controlling exposure without contacting hot water or electrical equipment.

Use the [water-heater emergency guide](/emergency) for safe next steps. A leaking pressure vessel is not repaired by tightening an external fitting.

### Signs of overheating, steam, violent discharge, or electrical danger

Do not operate controls, open panels, test a temperature-and-pressure relief valve, or approach wet electrical equipment in an attempt to diagnose the problem. Move people away and contact emergency services or a qualified professional as the situation requires.

## Changes that deserve an inspection

These signs are less dramatic. They still matter because a changed pattern contains more information than a generic age range.

### The hot-water supply has changed

The shower turns cold sooner. Recovery takes longer. Temperature moves up and down. A tankless unit loses output when another fixture opens. A heat-pump water heater begins relying on a different mode.

Those changes can point toward demand, settings, operating mode, a fixture problem, scale or sediment, a failed component, or another issue. Start with the [running-out-of-hot-water guide](/resources/running-out-of-hot-water). Record whether the problem affects one fixture or the whole home and whether it appeared suddenly.

### The unit shuts down or shows an error

Write down the exact code and model number. Then use the current manufacturer manual or official support channel for that model.

An error code is not a generic article topic. The same characters can mean different things across brands and models, and the correct response can depend on fuel, venting, electrical, combustion, water flow, or sensor conditions. Do not reset a recurring safety fault until the manufacturer’s instructions and a qualified professional support that action.

### Water appears discolored or smells different

First determine whether the change affects hot water only, cold water too, one fixture, or the entire home. That distinction can separate a water-heater question from a supply, piping, drain, or fixture question.

Odor and discoloration do not prove an anode rod, corrosion, or bacterial issue. Water chemistry, treatment, piping, stagnation, and heater conditions can overlap. Record the pattern and use the exact product guidance. The [anode-rod guide](/resources/water-heater-anode-rod) and [hard-water guide](/resources/hard-water-water-heater) explain the equipment side without claiming a remote diagnosis.

### New noise appears

Popping, rumbling, ticking, fan noise, burner noise, and flow noise do not all mean the same thing. Some sounds are part of normal operation. Others may change with scale, sediment, pressure, thermal expansion, a fan, a pump, a burner, or a component fault.

Record when the sound occurs and whether performance changed at the same time. Do not remove covers or work around live gas or electricity to locate it.

### Corrosion, moisture, or staining becomes visible

Photograph the location from the outside. Include the top connections, base, drain-pan area, nearby piping, and any stain path you can see safely.

Surface corrosion on one component does not prove that the tank is leaking. It does justify learning where the moisture came from before approving a repair.

## What to record before calling for service

A useful service call begins with evidence the technician can verify.

- Manufacturer, model, and serial number.
- Approximate installation date and service history.
- Fuel or power type.
- The exact symptom, when it began, and whether it changed suddenly.
- Whether one fixture or the whole home is affected.
- Any displayed code, alarm, visible water, corrosion, or staining.
- Recent changes in household use, plumbing work, utility service, settings, or operating mode.
- Exterior photos taken without opening panels or moving unsafe equipment.

This list does not diagnose the heater. It reduces guesswork.

## Warning sign or replacement verdict?

Most warning signs lead to one of three next steps: emergency action, qualified inspection, or a household-demand check. They do not automatically lead to replacement.

Once the failure is identified, use the [repair-or-replace guide](/resources/repair-or-replace-water-heater). It considers tank or heat-exchanger condition, repair scope, age, warranty, repeated failures, damage exposure, and whether the current system still fits.

If the unit is working but old, the [water-heater lifespan guide](/resources/how-long-water-heaters-last) helps turn age into a planning decision without pretending it is an expiration date.
